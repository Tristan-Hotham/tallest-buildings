The world's tallest building

For almost four thousand years the tallest thing people had built was a tomb in Egypt. Then came the spires of Lincoln, Stralsund and Strasbourg, the skyscrapers of New York and Chicago, and since 1998 the towers of Asia. Who held the record, for how long, and where the world's tall buildings stand today.

Dr Tristan Hotham · Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: English Wikipedia; Wikidata; Natural Earth

Ask which is the tallest building in the world and the answer, since 2010, is easy: the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, {{records.burj_h|int}} m. Ask which was the tallest building in 1500, or 1890, or 1970, and the answer depends on what you call a building, how you measure it and which spire was still standing. I followed the title through Wikipedia's history of the record, from the Great Pyramid of Giza to the cathedrals of medieval Europe, the race up Manhattan and the move to Asia, and put every holder on a map beside {{wd.n_counted|int}} of today's tall buildings from Wikidata and Wikipedia.

The record has moved between {{records.n_cities|int}} cities. Lincoln held it longest, then Strasbourg. For a century it belonged to the United States. Since 1998 it has been in Asia, and the next holder is already rising in Jeddah.

    02 · The recordFrom the pyramid to the Burj

    Here is every holder of the title, drawn to the same scale of height in the order they took it. Filled shapes are buildings in the sense Wikipedia's history uses, which counts churches and cathedrals; hatched shapes are structures that held the overall record but were not buildings. The outline on the right is Jeddah Tower, which the List page shows under construction at a planned {{uc.jeddah_h|int}} m.

    Every tallest building, to scale

    Heights from the Wikipedia tables; outlines are simplified sketches. Hover for the years each held the title.

    The Great Pyramid of Giza, {{records.pyr_h|f1}} m when new, was the tallest structure in the world for over 3,800 years, about {{records.pyr_years|int}} if it was finished around 2560 BC, as Wikidata gives. But it is a tomb, not a building, and Wikipedia's history says the tallest buildings before the 14th century cannot be determined. The first building on its list is London's Old St Paul's Cathedral ({{records.stpaul_h|int}} m, 13th century to 1311), overtaken by Lincoln Cathedral, whose spire reached {{records.lincoln_h|f1}} m in 1311. Lincoln's spire stood for {{records.lincoln_years|int}} years until it collapsed in 1549, the longest reign of any building.

    Collapse and fire then handed the title around northern Europe: to St Mary's in Stralsund, to Beauvais Cathedral (whose tower fell four years later), back to Stralsund until its bell tower burned in 1647, and then to Strasbourg Cathedral, which held it for {{records.stras_years|int}} years at {{records.strasbourg_h|int}} m, the shortest record-holder of all. Hamburg, Rouen, Cologne and Ulm followed in a burst of 19th century spires. Ulm Minster, {{records.ulm_h|f1}} m in 1890, was the last church to hold the record.

    Then the skyscraper. Philadelphia City Hall took the title in 1894, and from 1908 to 1973 it never left Manhattan: the Singer Building, the Metropolitan Life Tower, the Woolworth Building, 40 Wall Street and the Chrysler Building (both in 1930), the Empire State Building, which held it for {{records.esb_years|int}} years, and the World Trade Center. Chicago's Sears Tower took it in 1973. In 1998 the Petronas Towers in Kuala Lumpur ended a century of American records, Taipei 101 followed in 2004, and in 2010 the Burj Khalifa beat Taipei 101 by {{records.burj_jump_m|int}} m, {{records.burj_jump_pct|pc}}, the biggest jump in the record's history.

    How long each city held the record

    Count the years and the medieval cathedrals dominate: churches held the title for {{records.church_years|int}} of the {{records.span_years|int}} years since 1311 ({{records.church_pct|pc}}). Lincoln ({{records.lincoln_years|int}} years), Strasbourg ({{records.stras_years|int}}) and Stralsund ({{records.stral_years|int}}) lead, ahead of New York ({{records.nyc_years|int}}), Chicago ({{records.chi_years|int}}) and Dubai ({{records.dubai_years|int}} so far). Wikipedia's introduction says New York held the record for {{records.intro_nyc|int}} years and Chicago for {{records.intro_chi|int}}, but its own table gives {{records.nyc_years|int}} and {{records.chi_years|int}}; I use the table.

    03 · Buildings, towers and mastsWhat counts as a building?

    "Tallest building" sounds simple. It is not, and the two Wikipedia pages behind this study use different rules, so I say which one each part of the page uses.

    History of the record

    Wikipedia's 'History of the world's tallest buildings' calls a building something regularly inhabited or occupied, and "treats churches and cathedrals as buildings", although much of their height is in spires. Heights follow the CTBUH's default: the architectural top, including spires but not antennas, masts or flagpoles.

    Today's tallest

    Wikipedia's 'List of tallest buildings' counts enclosed structures with continuously occupiable floors, at least 350 m to the highest architectural feature, completed or architecturally topped out: {{list350.n|int}} buildings. Radio towers and other non-building structures are left out.

    Structures

    The Great Pyramid, the Washington Monument ({{records.wm_h|int}} m, 1884), the Eiffel Tower ({{records.eiffel_h|int}} m, 1889) and later the radio and television masts were the tallest structures but not buildings: you cannot live or work up there. Since 1884 the tallest structure has also been the tallest building only from 1930 to 1954, and again since 2010.

    Even the buildings argue. In 1930 the Chrysler Building secretly assembled its spire inside the building and hoisted it to beat 40 Wall Street, whose top floor was higher. In 1996 the Petronas Towers were ranked above the Sears Tower because their spires count and the Sears Tower's antennas do not. And the medieval heights are uncertain: Lincoln's {{records.lincoln_h|f1}} m is "disputed by some, but accepted by most sources", and Christopher Wren put Old St Paul's spire at 140 m, not {{records.stpaul_h|int}} m. The dots on the map come from a third source, Wikidata, which I cleaned as described in section 07.

    04 · The boomAsia rises

    For most of the 20th century tall buildings were an American story. In 1930, says Wikipedia, 99 of the world's 100 tallest buildings were in North America. Among the {{wd.n200|int}} buildings of 200 m or more that I could check in Wikidata, {{wd.na_pre1990_pct|pc}} of those finished before 1990 are in North America. Then Asia takes over: its share of new 200 m buildings rises from {{wd.asia_share.1980|pc}} in the 1980s to {{wd.asia_share.1990|pc}} in the 1990s and {{wd.asia_share.2010|pc}} in the 2010s, the busiest decade by far, with {{wd.tot.2010|int}} completions.

    05 · Where they are nowThe skyscraper cities

    Wikidata, after my checks, lists the most buildings of 200 m or more in {{wd.cities.0.city|raw}} ({{wd.cities.0.n|int}}), {{wd.cities.1.city|raw}} ({{wd.cities.1.n|int}}) and {{wd.cities.2.city|raw}} ({{wd.cities.2.n|int}}), then {{wd.cities.3.city|raw}} ({{wd.cities.3.n|int}}) and {{wd.cities.4.city|raw}} ({{wd.cities.4.n|int}}). That ranking is a ranking of records as much as of buildings. Wikidata is patchy for China: {{list350.missing_cn|int}} of the List's {{list350.china_hk|int}} buildings of 350 m or more in China and Hong Kong ({{list350.missing_cn_pct|pc}}) are missing from the extract, against {{list350.missing_other|int}} of the other {{list350.n_other|int}} ({{list350.missing_other_pct|pc}}), and the gaps are surely wider lower down. The complete list of 350 m buildings is more trustworthy for the very top, and it puts {{list350.cities.0.city|raw}} first with {{list350.cities.0.n|int}}, then {{list350.cities.1.city|raw}} ({{list350.cities.1.n|int}}) and {{list350.cities.2.city|raw}} ({{list350.cities.2.n|int}}).

    06 · What comes nextA kilometre in Jeddah

    {{uc.jeddah_h|int}} m

    Jeddah Tower, Saudi Arabia: planned architectural height {{uc.jeddah_h|int}} m, {{uc.jeddah_floors|raw}} floors, planned completion {{uc.jeddah_planned|raw}}, listed under construction on Wikipedia's 'List of tallest buildings'. If it is finished as planned it will be {{uc.jeddah_over_burj_m|int}} m taller than the Burj Khalifa and the first building of a kilometre.

    The same page lists {{uc.n|int}} buildings of 350 m or more under construction. {{uc.asia|int}} of them are in Asia, {{uc.china|int}} in China alone, and two would pass 600 m: Jeddah Tower and Dubai's Burj Azizi ({{uc.azizi_h|int}} m, planned for {{uc.azizi_planned|raw}}). Only {{uc.usa|int}} is in the United States. I use only what the page says about their status: planned heights and dates change, and some towers stall for years.

    07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Record-holders. The two tables of Wikipedia's 'History of the world's tallest buildings' give each holder's years, city and height: {{records.n_church|int}} rows for churches and cathedrals and {{records.n_sky|int}} for skyscrapers ({{records.n_buildings|int}} buildings; St Mary's in Stralsund appears twice). The structures track (the pyramid, the churches, the Washington Monument, the Eiffel Tower, the Chrysler and Empire State Buildings, the masts, the Burj Khalifa) follows the page's text. Neither page dates the Great Pyramid, so I use Wikidata's date, 2560 BC, which fits the page's "over 3,800 years". Years held run from one record to the next, with 2026 as the end of the Burj Khalifa's reign so far. Positions come from Wikidata, except Lincoln and Beauvais cathedrals, entered by hand, and the original World Trade Center, shown on its site.

    Today's buildings. The Wikidata extract holds {{wd.n_items|int}} structures with a height of 100 or more. Heights came without units, and many are feet or centimetres, so I kept only buildings (skyscrapers, offices, hotels, homes, churches and other occupied buildings: {{wd.drop_notbuilding|int}} towers, bridges, dams, memorials and the like left out), dropped {{wd.drop_dead|int}} unfinished or destroyed ones, {{wd.drop_830|int}} taller than the Burj Khalifa and {{wd.drop_noyear|int}} without a year. Buildings of 350 m or more had to be on the List page's complete table, which then supplied their height, year and city ({{wd.drop_350|int}} were not and were dropped). In the United States, where Wikidata often gives feet as metres, I kept a height under 350 m only if the CORGIS skyscrapers dataset, taken from the CTBUH's Skyscraper Center, had the same building within 8% ({{wd.n_corgis_ok|int}} did; {{wd.n_corgis_bad|int}} disagreed and {{wd.n_unchecked_us|int}} could not be checked, so they are not counted). Outside the United States I kept heights as given, apart from {{wd.drop_review|int}} that were plainly feet. Cities are the largest Natural Earth place within 40 km.

    Churches. The {{wd.religious|int}} churches, mosques and temples of 100 m or more in the extract are in the data file but not on the map or in the counts, because Wikidata dates them by their founding, not by when they reached their height.

    What this cannot say. Wikidata is not a census of skyscrapers. It is fullest for famous buildings and thinnest for China's, so the counts in sections 04 and 05 are counts of records, and some heights outside the United States will still be wrong. Leaving out unchecked American records lowers North America's share: counting all of them, Asia's share of new 200 m buildings in the 2010s would be {{wd.asia_share_us.2010|pc}} rather than {{wd.asia_share.2010|pc}}. Years are opening dates where Wikidata has them ({{wd.ysrc_open|int}} buildings), otherwise inception ({{wd.ysrc_inc|int}}), which is sometimes the start of construction.
